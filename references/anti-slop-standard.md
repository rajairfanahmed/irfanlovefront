# No-AI-Slop Standard

## Purpose
Establish concrete rules for avoiding generic, interchangeable AI-generated design. Every visual and interaction choice must serve the specific product, content, and users rather than follow a generic formula.

---

## The Problem
A significant proportion of AI-generated designs share a recognizable template: oversized gradient text, floating blur shapes, repeated rounded cards, neon accents, glass surfaces, centered hero layouts, or decorative 3D objects. These are not categorically forbidden. Their use must be intentional and suitable.

Equally problematic: replacing one default formula with another. A design that always uses an asymmetric split hero, always removes cards, always applies brutalist typography, or always adds a terminal aesthetic is just as formulaic as the template it replaced.

---

## Anti-Slop Checklist

### 1. No default hero sections
Every hero section must be designed for the specific product. No interchangeable gradient headings, vague taglines, or decorative elements that could apply to any product. However, a centered hero is not inherently wrong. Use it when centered composition is the strongest choice for the content.

### 2. No unexplained decorative elements
No floating blobs, abstract shapes, decorative topology graphics, fake telemetry panels, or decorative patterns unless they serve a clear purpose in the visual concept. If an element is removed and the design is unchanged, it should not have been there.

### 3. No default card grids
Cards should reflect actual content relationships, not be used as a default layout for every collection of items. But cards are legitimate when separation genuinely helps the user. Do not reject cards as a rule any more than you should default to them.

### 4. No fabricated social proof
No made-up testimonials, fake statistics, invented company logos, unverified ratings, generated user photos, fabricated usage figures, unsupported proficiency labels, or invented subscriber metrics.

### 5. No generic copy
Content should be specific to the product. No "Lorem ipsum," "Welcome to our platform," "We're revolutionizing the way you...," or interchangeable marketing language. Avoid formulaic AI phrasing, em dashes in ordinary prose, repetitive sentence patterns, hollow claims, and phrases that could belong to any product.

### 6. No gratuitous surface treatments
Use glassmorphism, gradients, shadows, rounded corners, and other surface treatments when they support hierarchy, grouping, or brand identity. Not as visual filler. Do not assume that a particular combination of light palette, rounded rectangles, subtle shadows, and a black primary button constitutes universal premium design.

### 7. No arbitrary icon collections
Icons must be relevant, consistent in style, and recognizable. No random icons chosen because they look decorative. Where official brand SVGs or vector icons exist in the project, use them instead of generic substitutes.

### 8. No decorative motion without purpose
Animation must communicate something: state changes, transitions, attention, feedback, or spatial relationships. Not arbitrary entrance effects, excessive parallax, ornamental cursor effects, repeated reveal animations, or blanket transition utilities. Inspect the installed stack before adding motion dependencies.

### 9. No forced layout symmetry or asymmetry
Layouts should reflect content relationships. Do not arrange elements symmetrically because it looks "clean," and do not force asymmetry because symmetry seems generic. Choose the composition that serves the content.

### 10. No template composition
Each product deserves a composition appropriate to its content, hierarchy, and purpose. Do not repeat the same layout structure across unrelated products. Do not repeat the same composition across every page of a single product — maintain a coherent visual language while allowing each page to respond to its own content and task.

### 11. No fake functional elements
A live clock may show a real clock. A terminal must execute meaningful supported actions or be clearly presented as a nonfunctional visual demonstration. System health, latency, transaction logs, subscriber metrics, and similar data must not be fabricated and presented as real. An architecture diagram should depict the project's actual architecture, not a fictional one.

---

## What "Intentional Design" Means

Every significant visual element should have a defensible answer to:
- What specific purpose does it serve?
- Would removing it reduce the design's effectiveness?
- Is it appropriate for this specific product and audience?
- Does it contribute to the hierarchy or composition?
- Does it arise from the brief or from a reasonable creative interpretation of it?

---

## Design Thesis Requirement

Before implementation, establish a concise design thesis that explains the visual idea in practical terms. The thesis should address layout, typographic character, visual hierarchy, image treatment, spatial rhythm, component language, and motion as connected decisions. The concept must arise from the brief or from reasonable creative interpretation of it. This is not a document to present to the user unless asked; it is an internal quality standard.

---

## Permitted Use of Common Patterns

Gradients, glassmorphism, cards, hero sections, centered layouts, rounded corners, animations, shadows, and popular visual treatments are all legitimate tools. The standard is not prohibition but intention. Use them when they contribute to a coherent, product-specific result. Avoid them when they are the default choice regardless of context.

Symmetry, centered composition, three-column grids, and familiar UI conventions are valid when they are the strongest choice. Do not treat anti-slop as a universal ban on common patterns.

**Foundational principle:** A specific product designed by a thoughtful professional should never look like it could have been any other product. Achieve this through informed choices, not through rigid pattern prohibitions.
